Summary: Observation, Experience, and Co-Arising
I began by noticing that my understanding of quantum physics had destabilized simple ideas about observation and reality. In measurement, the observer is never outside the system: the instrument is part of what is measured. The measured arises from the measurement. This insight generalizes beyond physics. Our senses are instruments; perception is interaction, not passive viewing. There is no standpoint outside the system from which reality can be observed “as it is.”
This led me to reconsider the idea of an observer altogether. If observation always involves participation, then there is no external witness—no privileged vantage point inside or outside the world. Experience happens, but not to a fixed observer standing apart from it.
I initially expressed this through the familiar “tree falling in the forest” question. I refined my position to avoid confusion: if a tree falls in a forest and I am not there, there is no tree and no forest to me. This is not a claim about what ultimately exists, but a statement about experience. “Tree” and “forest” are not things I encounter outside perception; they arise only in experience, memory, or inference. Outside experience, those categories simply do not apply to me.
Going further, I saw that even the “me” who might be present is not solid or independent. There is no fixed self that stands somewhere and observes. The tree, the forest, and the sense of “me” arise together under certain causes and conditions. None of them precede the others. Subject and object are not separate entities that later interact; they are aspects of a single, conditionally arisen event. Without me, as observer, there is no tree. Without the tree there can be no observer.
From this perspective, there are not one true tree, one true forest, or one true self. There are innumerable trees, forests, and “me”s—not as separate substances, but as endlessly variable configurations of conditions. No configuration is final or privileged. “Infinite” here means open-ended and unfinalizable, not a countable infinity of things.
I explored the idea of a “field of possibilities” as a way of speaking about this openness, but recognized that this too is only a mental model. Like mathematics, it is an abstraction we use to orient ourselves. There is no evidence that such a field exists as a solid underlying substrate. To avoid reifying a concept, I am content to treat it as illustrative at best—or to drop it entirely. If something cannot be shown to be more than a mental construct, there is no need to build metaphysics on it.
This led to a clear boundary with quantum theory itself. Quantum physics does not prove any philosophical position. It neither establishes a metaphysical ground nor validates spiritual conclusions. Its real contribution, for me, is that it undermines naïve realism and cautions against assuming simple, observer-independent objects. Beyond that, philosophy must stand on its own, and experience must speak for itself.
I also recognized that none of this requires extraordinary states or mystical experiences. There is no need to seek something beyond ordinary life. Normal experience—exactly as it unfolds—is sufficient. What matters is not discovering a hidden reality, but understanding how suffering arises within experience and how it can be eased.
In the end, I arrive at a position that refuses both extremes. I do not posit a fundamental observer, a universal consciousness, or an underlying substance. I also do not fall into nihilism. Experience happens. Appearances arise conditionally. No solid self or world stands behind them. There may be no ultimate ground at all—and that is acceptable. Meaning and responsibility do not depend on metaphysical certainty; they arise within the same conditions as suffering and compassion.
What remains is simple and sufficient: co-arising, impermanence, and the practical task of reducing suffering within what appears.
